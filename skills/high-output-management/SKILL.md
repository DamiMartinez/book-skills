# High Output Management

Apply this skill to reframe management, delegation, meeting, and planning problems around Andrew Grove's central idea: a manager's output is not their own personal output, but the output of their organization plus the output of the neighboring organizations they influence. Every management activity should be judged by whether it raises that output.

## Core concepts

**Managerial output & leverage** — A manager's output = the output of their team + the output of adjacent teams under their influence. Managerial leverage is the ratio of that output to the manager's own time/effort invested. High-leverage activities: things that affect many people for a long time (training, a well-run meeting that improves how a team works, a decision that shapes future decisions). When evaluating how a manager should spend time, ask which activities have the highest leverage, not which feel most productive.

**Production-line thinking** — Model any process (including "soft" work like hiring or onboarding) like Grove's breakfast factory: map the steps, find the limiting/bottleneck step, and add value as early in the process as possible (cheaper to catch a defect early than late). Useful for any repeated workflow the user wants to improve, not just literal manufacturing.

**Indicators & monitoring, not control** — Track a small set of measures that predict output before it fully materializes (leading indicators), paired with at least one counterbalancing measure so optimizing one doesn't quietly wreck another (e.g. speed + quality, output + customer satisfaction). Indicators exist to prompt corrective action, not to punish — the manager should still visit the actual place of work ("management by walking around") rather than trust numbers alone.

**Task-relevant maturity (TRM) & delegation style** — How directive vs. hands-off a manager should be with a given person depends on that person's maturity *for that specific task* (skill + experience + motivation on it), not their seniority in general. Low TRM → more structured, closer supervision; high TRM → delegate and get out of the way. Monitor delegated work without taking it back — Grove distinguishes monitoring (checking output/process) from meddling (redoing the work yourself).

**One-on-ones** — The subordinate owns the agenda; the manager's job is to listen, surface problems early, and teach/coach. Frequency and depth should scale with the person's task-relevant maturity and the complexity of what they're working on — struggling or new employees need more time, not less.

**Meetings as the medium of managerial work** — Distinguish *process-oriented* meetings (regular, e.g. staff meetings, 1:1s — knowledge-sharing) from *mission-oriented* meetings (ad hoc, called to solve a specific problem or make a specific decision — should end with a decision, not just discussion). A meeting is a manager's tool, not overhead; a bad meeting culture is a leverage problem.

**Decision-making process** — Good decisions go through: free and open discussion (everyone's view genuinely heard) → clear decision (even if not consensus) → full support from everyone afterward, including those who disagreed. Watch for a team stuck because it skipped straight to "support" without real discussion, or that re-litigates decisions instead of committing.

**Hybrid organizations & dual reporting** — Real orgs are neither purely functional nor purely mission-based; people often have a primary reporting line plus a dotted-line relationship elsewhere. This is normal and not inherently dysfunctional — the fix for confusion is clarity about which hat someone is wearing for a given decision, not eliminating the structure.

**Planning / the production gap** — Planning starts from the gap between what the environment will demand and what current momentum would produce, then asks what actions close that gap starting today. A plan should produce today's action items, not just a forecast document.

**Objective setting (iMBO)** — Objectives should be few, clear, and paired with key results that are measurable evidence the objective was met (the direct ancestor of OKRs). Push back on objective lists that are really just task lists, or key results that can't actually verify the objective.

## How to apply this

When the user brings a management, delegation, meeting, planning, or team-output problem, don't just give generic advice — route it through Grove's lens:

1. **Reframe the goal as output/leverage** — whose output is actually at stake, and what's the highest-leverage way to affect it (a decision, a piece of training, a process fix) rather than the manager doing the work themselves?
2. **If it's a delegation question** — ask (or infer) the person's task-relevant maturity for *this specific task* and recommend a directive/hands-off style accordingly. Flag if the plan risks meddling (redoing delegated work) instead of monitoring.
3. **If it's a process/workflow problem** — map it like a production line: steps, the limiting step, where to catch problems earliest and cheapest.
4. **If it's about measuring progress** — propose 1-2 leading indicators plus a counterbalancing measure, and check any metrics already proposed aren't just lagging vanity numbers.
5. **If it's a meeting problem** — classify it as process- vs. mission-oriented and fix accordingly (mission-oriented meetings need a decision-owner and should end with a decision).
6. **If it's a decision that's stalling or getting re-litigated** — check whether it actually went through free discussion → clear decision → committed support, and name which step got skipped.
7. **If it's about objectives/OKRs** — check the objectives are few and clear, and each key result is actually measurable evidence, not a disguised task list.

Keep it practical and specific to the user's situation — apply the relevant concept(s), don't recite the whole framework every time.

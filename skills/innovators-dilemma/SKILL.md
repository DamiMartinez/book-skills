# The Innovator's Dilemma

Apply Clayton M. Christensen's explanation of why well-managed firms can lose to emerging competitors: the customers, economics, and decision rules that make the core business successful can prevent investment in a different value network.

## Core concepts

**Sustaining vs. disruptive innovation** — Sustaining innovations improve performance along dimensions established customers already value; they can be incremental or radical. Disruptive technologies initially underperform on mainstream measures but offer a different advantage, such as simplicity, convenience, smaller size, or lower cost, to an overlooked market. Classify an innovation relative to specific customers and incumbents. Novelty, technical sophistication, rapid growth, and competitive damage alone do not establish disruption.

**Value networks** — Customers, suppliers, channels, cost structures, and expected margins shape what a firm considers valuable and profitable. The same technology can be attractive in one network and unattractive in another. Map who will buy it, which attributes matter to them, and how the business can make money serving them before judging its potential.

**Performance trajectories and oversupply** — Technical performance can improve faster than customers' ability to use it. Once established products exceed what a segment needs, buyers may favor convenience, reliability, or price. Compare the entrant's improvement trajectory with the segment's actual requirements; initially inferior performance can become sufficient. Treat the timing and feasibility of that crossing as hypotheses, not inevitable outcomes.

**Resource dependence and rational rejection** — Customers and investors influence which projects receive resources, including through everyday sales and budgeting decisions. Small, low-margin opportunities lose against established accounts even when leaders support innovation. Identify the allocation rules and incentives that would starve the initiative instead of attributing the problem to managerial ignorance or a lack of courage.

**Small markets and organizational scale** — An emerging market can sustain a small venture while being too small to satisfy a large company's growth requirements. Match the initiative to an organization whose success can be meaningful at that scale. Avoid requiring a speculative new market to meet the core business's revenue targets before learning whether it exists.

**Discovery-driven planning** — Reliable forecasts are unavailable for markets that do not yet exist. Make assumptions explicit, use affordable market probes to discover customers and applications, and preserve resources for changes in direction. Judge early progress by what has been learned about demand and economics, then increase commitments as evidence improves.

**Resources, processes, and values** — People, cash, and technology do not by themselves establish organizational fit. Processes determine how work gets done; values determine which opportunities receive priority. Assess whether the existing organization can support the required economics and decisions. An autonomous unit or acquisition can help when those processes and values conflict, provided it has appropriate customers, cost structure, authority, and protected resources; a separate reporting line alone is insufficient.

## How to apply this

1. **Frame the decision and actors.** Identify the incumbent, entrant or initiative, customer segments, and the investment or competitive response under consideration. Separate known facts from assumptions; ask for missing information only when it would change the recommendation.
2. **Test the disruption hypothesis.** Compare mainstream performance with the alternative's distinct advantages and initial customers. Explain whether the evidence supports sustaining innovation, a plausible disruptive trajectory, or an unresolved classification. Do not force every threat into the disruption framework.
3. **Map the value networks and trajectories.** Compare buying criteria, channels, prices, costs, and margins. Identify overserved customers or emerging applications and what would need to improve before the entrant could serve mainstream demand. State what evidence would weaken that thesis.
4. **Diagnose the allocation conflict.** Name the specific customers, approval criteria, incentives, processes, or growth targets that favor the core business. For an entrant, examine why incumbents might rationally leave its initial market alone and what could draw a response.
5. **Choose an organizational response.** Recommend strengthening the core, exploring within it, or creating/acquiring an autonomous business according to the actual process and value conflicts. Explain the tradeoff and define decision rights, appropriate scale, and funding where separation is warranted.
6. **Propose a bounded learning plan.** Specify the first customer/application test, spending limit, evidence of willingness to pay and viable economics, and a review point for continuing, changing direction, or stopping. Track adoption and performance against the target segment's needs before assuming an upmarket move.

Keep the response tied to the user's decision. Use disruption as an explanatory lens rather than a prediction of inevitable incumbent failure. Avoid invented quotations, page references, and forecasts. When using later refinements such as the explicit low-end/new-market distinction, identify them as developments of the theory rather than terminology unique to the original book.
